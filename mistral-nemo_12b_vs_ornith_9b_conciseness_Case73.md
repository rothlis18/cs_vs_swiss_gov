In the Credit Suisse collapse, the Swiss government's use of a bridge bank structure and rapid acquisition by UBS, rather than a strict bail-in under Article 83 of the Swiss Banking Act, effectively inverted the typical hierarchy of loss absorption. Here's how:

1. **Bridge Bank Mechanism**: A bridge bank is a temporary institution created to maintain the operations of a failed bank while a buyer is found. In Credit Suisse's case, UBS took over the bridge bank, absorbing its assets and liabilities. This mechanism allowed UBS to cherry-pick the most valuable assets, leaving behind riskier ones.

2. **Shareholder Wipe-out**: Shareholders of Credit Suisse were wiped out as part of the deal. This is unusual because in a typical bail-in scenario, shareholders are the first to absorb losses, followed by unsecured bondholders and then depositors. However, in this case, shareholders were wiped out before any other party.

3. **Bondholder Restructuring**: Unsecured bondholders of Credit Suisse faced forced restructurings, with some reports suggesting they could face significant losses. This is a departure from the typical hierarchy where bondholders are supposed to absorb losses before depositors.

This outcome illustrates the tension between legal resolution frameworks and pragmatic imperatives of systemic stability, particularly for "too big to fail" institutions:

- **Legal Hierarchy**: The Swiss Banking Act (Article 83) follows the typical hierarchy of loss absorption, with shareholders and bondholders absorbing losses before depositors. This is designed to protect depositors, who are often seen as innocent victims.

- **Pragmatic Imperatives**: When a bank is deemed too big to fail, the focus shifts to maintaining systemic stability. This can lead to deviations from the legal hierarchy. In Credit Suisse's case, the Swiss government prioritized maintaining confidence in the banking system and preventing contagion effects, even if it meant deviating from the legal loss absorption order.

In essence, the bridge bank mechanism allowed for a pragmatic solution that prioritized systemic stability over strict adherence to the legal loss absorption hierarchy. This inversion of priority highlights the tension between legal frameworks and practical considerations when dealing with large, complex financial institutions.
