In the Credit Suisse collapse, the Swiss government facilitated a bridge bank structure and a rapid acquisition by UBS to maintain systemic stability, rather than executing a strict bail-in under Article 83 of the Swiss Banking Act. This decision effectively inverted the typical hierarchy of loss absorption, allowing shareholders to be wiped out while bondholders faced forced restructurings. Here's how this outcome illustrates the tension between legal resolution frameworks and pragmatic imperatives of systemic stability:

1. **Legal Hierarchy of Loss Absorption**: According to the Basel III framework and the Swiss Banking Act, unsecured bondholders are supposed to absorb losses before shareholders in case of a bank's failure. This is to ensure that creditors bear the risk of their investments, rather than taxpayers or depositors.

2. **Pragmatic Imperatives of Systemic Stability**: In the case of Credit Suisse, the Swiss government prioritized systemic stability over the strict application of the legal hierarchy. A disorderly collapse of Credit Suisse could have had significant spillover effects on other financial institutions and the broader economy. Therefore, the Swiss National Bank (SNB) and the Swiss Finance Ministry chose to facilitate a quick acquisition by UBS to prevent contagion.

3. **Bridge Bank Structure and Bail-in**: The bridge bank structure allowed UBS to acquire Credit Suisse's assets and liabilities, excluding certain risky assets. This effectively bypassed the legal hierarchy of loss absorption. Shareholders of Credit Suisse were wiped out, while bondholders were not immediately bailed in. Instead, they faced a forced restructuring, with their bonds converted into shares of the new entity.

4. **Tension Between Legal Frameworks and Pragmatism**: This outcome illustrates the tension between legal resolution frameworks and the pragmatic imperatives of systemic stability. While the law dictates that bondholders should absorb losses before shareholders, the reality of a too-big-to-fail institution collapsing can lead to exceptions. The Swiss government chose to prioritize systemic stability over the strict application of the legal hierarchy to prevent a disorderly collapse and maintain confidence in the Swiss banking system.

In essence, the Credit Suisse collapse demonstrates that while legal frameworks provide a clear hierarchy of loss absorption, pragmatic considerations of systemic stability can lead to deviations from these rules when a bank's failure threatens broader financial stability. This tension between legal frameworks and pragmatic imperatives is a recurring theme in the resolution of complex financial failures.
