In the Credit Suisse collapse, the Swiss government's use of a bridge bank structure and rapid acquisition by UBS, instead of a strict bail-in under Article 83 of the Swiss Banking Act, effectively inverted the typical loss absorption hierarchy. Here's how:

1. **Bridge Bank Mechanism**: A bridge bank is a temporary institution created to maintain the operations of a failed bank while a buyer is found. In Credit Suisse's case, UBS took over the bridge bank, absorbing its assets and liabilities. This mechanism allowed UBS to cherry-pick the most valuable assets, leaving behind riskier ones.

2. **Shareholder Wipe-out**: Shareholders of Credit Suisse were wiped out as part of the deal. This is unusual because, in a typical bail-in scenario, shareholders are the first to absorb losses, followed by unsecured bondholders and then depositors. However, in this case, shareholders were wiped out before any other party.

3. **Bondholder Haircut**: Unsecured bondholders did not face a complete wipe-out but did suffer a significant haircut (around 16 billion Swiss francs, or about 60% of their investment). This is a departure from the typical hierarchy where bondholders are supposed to absorb losses before depositors.

This outcome illustrates the tension between legal resolution frameworks and pragmatic imperatives of systemic stability, particularly for "too big to fail" institutions:

- **Legal Hierarchy**: The Swiss Banking Act's Article 83 dictates a clear hierarchy of loss absorption, with shareholders and bondholders absorbing losses before depositors. This is designed to protect small depositors and maintain market discipline.

- **Pragmatic Imperatives**: In practice, when a large, systemically important bank like Credit Suisse fails, governments often prioritize stability over legal hierarchy. This can lead to inversions in loss absorption, as seen here, to prevent contagion and maintain public confidence in the banking system.

In essence, while the law dictates one order of loss absorption, pragmatic concerns about systemic stability can lead to a different outcome, particularly for large, complex financial institutions. This tension between legal frameworks and practical considerations is a recurring theme in banking crises.
