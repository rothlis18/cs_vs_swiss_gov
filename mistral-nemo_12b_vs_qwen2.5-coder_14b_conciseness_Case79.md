In the Credit Suisse collapse, the Swiss government's use of a bridge bank structure and rapid acquisition by UBS, rather than a strict bail-in under Article 83 of the Swiss Banking Act, effectively inverted the typical hierarchy of loss absorption. Here's how:

1. **Bridge Bank Mechanism**: A bridge bank is a temporary bank created to facilitate the transfer of assets and liabilities from a failed bank to a healthy one. In Credit Suisse's case, UBS took on most of Credit Suisse's assets and liabilities, including its bonds and shares.

2. **Shareholder Wipe-out**: As part of the acquisition, Credit Suisse's shareholders were wiped out. They received one share in the new UBS entity for every 22.49 shares they held in Credit Suisse. This was a significant dilution, effectively wiping out their ownership.

3. **Bondholder Haircut**: While bondholders initially feared they would face significant losses, the bridge bank structure allowed them to be treated as liabilities transferred to UBS. This meant they were not immediately wiped out, but they did face a forced restructuring. UBS agreed to assume around CHF 16 billion of Credit Suisse's additional tier 1 bonds, but these bonds were converted into UBS shares, resulting in a significant haircut for bondholders.

This outcome illustrates the tension between legal resolution frameworks and systemic stability concerns:

- **Legal Hierarchy**: Under the bail-in rules of the Swiss Banking Act, unsecured bondholders are supposed to absorb losses before shareholders in the event of a bank's failure. This is to ensure that creditors, not taxpayers, bear the risk of a bank's failure.

- **Systemic Stability Imperative**: In the case of Credit Suisse, the Swiss government prioritized systemic stability over the legal hierarchy of loss absorption. They feared that a strict bail-in could lead to a loss of confidence in Credit Suisse, causing a wider banking crisis. Therefore, they facilitated a rapid acquisition by UBS to maintain stability, effectively inverting the typical loss absorption hierarchy.

This tension highlights the challenge policymakers face when dealing with large, complex financial institutions. While legal frameworks aim to ensure fair loss absorption, practical considerations of systemic stability can lead to deviations from these rules.
