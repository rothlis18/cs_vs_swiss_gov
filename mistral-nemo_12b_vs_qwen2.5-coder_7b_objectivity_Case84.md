In the Credit Suisse collapse, the Swiss government and UBS inverted the typical loss absorption hierarchy through a mechanism called "bail-out" instead of "bail-in". Here's how this worked:

1. **Bridge Bank Structure**: The Swiss government facilitated a bridge bank structure, which allowed UBS to acquire Credit Suisse's assets and liabilities. This structure enabled UBS to cherry-pick the most valuable assets while leaving behind the riskier ones.

2. **Shareholder Wipe-out**: As part of the acquisition, Credit Suisse's shareholders were wiped out. This is unusual because, in a typical bail-in scenario, shareholders are the first to absorb losses, followed by unsecured bondholders and then depositors.

3. **Bondholder Haircut**: While shareholders were wiped out, unsecured bondholders did not face a full bail-in. Instead, they faced a forced restructuring, with some reports suggesting a potential haircut of around 16%. This is a significant reduction compared to what they would have faced in a strict bail-in scenario.

This outcome illustrates the tension between legal resolution frameworks and systemic stability concerns, particularly for "too big to fail" institutions:

- **Legal Hierarchy**: The Swiss Banking Act (Article 83) dictates that in a bail-in, shareholders should absorb losses first, followed by unsecured bondholders. This is designed to protect depositors and maintain market discipline.

- **Pragmatic Imperatives**: In the Credit Suisse case, systemic stability concerns (i.e., preventing contagion effects on other banks and the broader economy) led to a deviation from the legal hierarchy. The Swiss government and UBS prioritized stabilizing the system over adhering strictly to the legal loss absorption order.

This tension highlights the challenge of balancing legal principles with practical considerations in managing financial crises. It also underscores the potential for governments to intervene and alter the loss absorption hierarchy when systemic stability is at risk.
