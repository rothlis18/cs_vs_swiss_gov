In the Credit Suisse-UBS bridge bank restructuring, the Swiss government and regulators effectively inverted the typical loss absorption hierarchy by using a mechanism called "bail-in" with certain conditions that protected bondholders while wiping out shareholders. Here's how this worked:

1. **Bridge Bank Structure**: The Swiss National Bank (SNB) facilitated a bridge bank structure, where UBS took over Credit Suisse's assets and liabilities, excluding certain risky assets. This allowed for a rapid acquisition and stabilization of Credit Suisse's operations.

2. **Bail-in with Conditions**: The Swiss government used a bail-in mechanism under Article 83 of the Swiss Banking Act, but with conditions that prioritized bondholders over shareholders. Here's how:

   - **Shareholder Wipe-out**: Shareholders of Credit Suisse were wiped out, as their shares were converted into UBS shares at a ratio of 1:22. This effectively diluted their ownership and wiped out their value.

   - **Bondholder Protection**: Unsecured bondholders were not wiped out but faced forced restructurings. The SNB guaranteed their claims up to 16 billion Swiss francs, and the remaining bondholders agreed to a 16 billion franc writedown. This protected bondholders from significant losses while shareholders bore the brunt of the losses.

This outcome illustrates the tension between legal resolution frameworks and systemic stability concerns, particularly for "too big to fail" institutions:

- **Legal Hierarchy**: The theoretical hierarchy of loss absorption dictates that unsecured bondholders should absorb losses before shareholders. This is based on the principle of "pari passu," where all creditors are treated equally.

- **Pragmatic Imperatives**: In practice, however, regulators often prioritize systemic stability over strict legal hierarchy. In this case, protecting bondholders (who are often institutional investors with significant influence) was seen as crucial for maintaining market confidence and preventing contagion effects.

- **Too Big to Fail**: The size and interconnectedness of Credit Suisse made its failure potentially systemic, leading regulators to prioritize stability over strict legal hierarchy. This is a common tension in resolving large financial institutions, where legal frameworks may not align with pragmatic needs.

In essence, the bridge bank structure and conditional bail-in allowed regulators to balance the legal hierarchy of loss absorption with the pragmatic imperatives of systemic stability, protecting bondholders while wiping out shareholders. This illustrates the complex trade-offs regulators must make when dealing with large, complex financial institutions.
