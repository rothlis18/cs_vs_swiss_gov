In the Credit Suisse restructuring, the Swiss government and UBS inverted the typical loss absorption hierarchy through a mechanism called "bail-out" instead of "bail-in". Here's how this worked:

1. **Bridge Bank Structure**: The Swiss government facilitated a bridge bank structure, where UBS took over Credit Suisse's assets and liabilities. This allowed Credit Suisse's operations to continue while a solution was found.

2. **Acquisition by UBS**: UBS acquired Credit Suisse at a price of CHF 3 billion, effectively wiping out Credit Suisse's shareholders. This is a reversal of the typical hierarchy, where shareholders are usually the first to absorb losses.

3. **Bondholders' Fate**: Bondholders, who should have borne losses before shareholders according to the Banking Act, faced a forced restructuring. They will receive UBS shares in exchange for their Credit Suisse bonds, but these shares are likely to be worth less than the bonds' face value.

This outcome illustrates the tension between legal resolution frameworks and systemic stability concerns:

- **Legal Framework (Bail-in)**: The Swiss Banking Act's Article 83 dictates that unsecured bondholders should absorb losses before shareholders in case of a bank's failure. This is designed to protect taxpayers and maintain market discipline.

- **Pragmatic Imperatives (Too Big to Fail)**: Credit Suisse was considered "too big to fail" due to its systemic importance. A bail-in could have caused significant market disruption and potentially led to a loss of confidence in Swiss banks. Therefore, the Swiss government chose a bail-out to maintain stability.

The tension lies in the fact that while the legal framework aims to maintain market discipline and fairness, the pragmatic imperative of systemic stability led to a deviation from this hierarchy. This raises questions about the fairness of the outcome and the effectiveness of the Banking Act in maintaining market discipline in the face of systemic stability concerns.
