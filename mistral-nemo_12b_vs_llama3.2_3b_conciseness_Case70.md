In the Credit Suisse collapse, the Swiss government and UBS inverted the typical loss absorption hierarchy through a mechanism called "bail-out" instead of "bail-in". Here's how this worked:

1. **Bridge Bank Structure**: The Swiss government facilitated a bridge bank structure, allowing UBS to acquire Credit Suisse's assets and liabilities. This structure enabled UBS to cherry-pick the most valuable assets, leaving behind the riskier ones.

2. **Shareholder Wipe-out**: As part of the deal, Credit Suisse's shareholders were wiped out. They received nothing, effectively absorbing all the losses. This is a reversal of the typical hierarchy, where shareholders are usually the last in line to absorb losses.

3. **Bondholder Haircut**: While bondholders were not wiped out, they did face a significant haircut (around 16 billion Swiss francs). This was a forced restructuring, as bondholders had no choice but to accept the deal to avoid further losses.

This outcome illustrates the tension between legal resolution frameworks and systemic stability concerns:

- **Legal Framework (Bail-in)**: According to Article 83 of the Swiss Banking Act, unsecured bondholders should absorb losses before shareholders in a bank failure. This is the legal, hierarchical order of loss absorption.

- **Pragmatic Imperatives (Too Big to Fail)**: However, when a bank is deemed "too big to fail", pragmatic concerns about systemic stability come into play. In Credit Suisse's case, the Swiss government and UBS prioritized maintaining systemic stability over adhering strictly to the legal hierarchy. They chose a bail-out over a bail-in to prevent contagion effects and maintain public confidence in the banking system.

In essence, the Credit Suisse collapse demonstrates that when faced with a too-big-to-fail scenario, legal frameworks can be bent or broken to maintain systemic stability, even if it means inverting the typical loss absorption hierarchy. This tension between legal frameworks and pragmatic imperatives is a recurring theme in financial crisis management.
